\title{Which Training Errors Are Dangerous for ASR?\\
A Controlled Audit of Failure Phenotypes Under Corrupted Supervision}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems are increasingly adapted with
pseudo-labels, automatic alignments, and other forms of weak supervision.
Training-data quality is usually summarized by an overall error rate, implicitly
treating different incorrect labels as equivalent. We ask whether the
\emph{structure} of erroneous supervision determines how an ASR model fails.
Using Whisper-large-v3 with LoRA adaptation, we hold corruption prevalence fixed
at 64\% and manipulate whether erroneous audio sources and targets are unique or
repeated. The resulting models exhibit sharply different failure phenotypes
under matched acoustic stress. Repeated-audio/unique-target corruption (RU)
produces fluent, predominantly non-repetitive high-error outputs: 78.0\% satisfy
our high-WER/high-Qwen-plausibility diagnostic and 76.0\% remain after excluding
repetition, while only 0.7\% of high-error outputs resemble deliberately
corrupted training targets. Unique-audio/repeated-target corruption (UR) shows
the opposite behavior: 61.3\% of stressed outputs contain repeated trigrams or
four-grams and 95.3\% of high-error outputs closely match corrupted targets
(mean nearest-target similarity 0.921). Repeated erroneous pairs (RR) instead
collapse toward a dominant generic output with 52.0\% Top-1 mass but no target
reuse, while unique-unique corruption (UU) is intermediate. External validation
on 4,990 human-annotated HALAS predictions confirms that WER alone does not
fully identify hallucination (ROC-AUC 0.761) and that repetition is a distinct
human-recognizable failure component. The results show that error prevalence
alone is insufficient for ASR data auditing: source repetition, target
repetition, and mismatch structure predict qualitatively different reliability
risks.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
automatic speech recognition, hallucination, label noise, weak supervision,
robustness, data auditing
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Modern ASR systems are commonly adapted to new domains, accents, speakers, and
languages using data that are not perfectly annotated. Large-scale pipelines
increasingly rely on pseudo-labels, automatically aligned transcripts, or other
weakly supervised audio--text pairs. Consequently, some erroneous supervision
is often unavoidable.

Such data are usually characterized by \emph{how much} error they contain: an
estimated pseudo-label accuracy, confidence threshold, or percentage of
mismatched examples. This scalar view ignores a second property: \emph{how the
errors are structured}. The same number of incorrect examples can consist of
independent one-off mismatches, duplicates of a small number of erroneous
pairs, repeated audio receiving inconsistent labels, or many unrelated
recordings sharing the same wrong transcript. These cases provide very
different learning signals.

This distinction matters because ASR failures are also heterogeneous. A model
can make local recognition errors, generate fluent content weakly supported by
the signal, repeat phrases within an output, collapse many inputs to the same
generic response, or reproduce erroneous targets seen during adaptation. WER
collapses these behaviors into a single edit-distance measure. Recent work on
ASR hallucination likewise shows that severe recognition error and unsupported
generation should not be treated as interchangeable
\cite{koenecke2024careless,atwany2025lost,baranski2025investigation,sridhar25_interspeech,imai-etal-2025-evaluating,baranski2026halas}.

Our earlier study introduced controlled source--target mismatch patterns and
provided initial evidence that different corruption structures can induce
different hallucination behavior \cite{frieske2024hallucinations}. The present
work revisits that question with Whisper-large-v3 and reframes it as a practical
data-auditing problem:

\begin{quote}
\emph{What kinds of training-data errors are dangerous for ASR, and how should
practitioners audit them?}
\end{quote}

We independently manipulate source and target repetition while fixing the
corruption rate at 64\%. We then evaluate all models on the same held-out
utterances under clean input and one matched acoustic-stress condition. Rather
than proposing a universal hallucination detector, we measure complementary
failure signatures: WER, LM-relative plausibility, within-output repetition,
cross-utterance concentration, and direct similarity to the deliberately
corrupted training targets.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we show that matched corruption
prevalence and training recipe produce qualitatively different ASR failure
phenotypes depending on the structure of erroneous supervision. Second, we
separate \emph{target-attractor} failures, which are directly traceable to
corrupted labels, from fluent high-error failures with negligible target reuse.
Third, we translate these controlled findings into concrete data- and
model-level audit recommendations, and use HALAS \cite{baranski2026halas} to
ground the distinction between lexical error, hallucination, and repetition on
natural speech.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{ASR hallucination and failure analysis}
Generative ASR can produce fabricated, repetitive, or weakly grounded text under
non-speech input, difficult recording conditions, distribution shift, and
acoustic degradation
\cite{koenecke2024careless,atwany2025lost,baranski2025investigation,sridhar25_interspeech,imai-etal-2025-evaluating,Zhang2024}.
HALAS \cite{baranski2026halas} provides human span-level annotations of
naturally occurring hallucination and looping across modern ASR systems. Recent
work has also explored hallucination detection and mitigation from text,
decoder signals, or post-processing. Our focus is upstream of detection: we ask
which \emph{properties of adaptation errors} create different downstream
failure regimes.

\subsection{Noisy supervision and robustness}
Noise injection and robustness studies establish that degraded inputs and
imperfect supervision can substantially affect recognition
\cite{Fetic2021TopicMR,morales2007adding,Iwamoto2022HowBA}. However, a scalar
noise rate does not describe whether erroneous supervision is duplicated,
concentrated on a few targets, or inconsistent for repeated sources. Our
controlled taxonomy isolates these sequence-level structures while preserving
the same total training-set size. This allows us to ask whether two datasets
with identical corruption prevalence pose different reliability risks.

\section{Controlled Training-Error Audit}
\subsection{Corruption taxonomy}
We construct four corruption structures by independently varying whether the
audio source and erroneous transcript are unique (U) or repeated (R).
Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} summarizes the interventions and their practical
interpretation. The practical analogues are illustrative: the controlled
conditions isolate structural properties rather than claiming to reproduce a
specific pseudo-labeling pipeline exactly.

\midrule
UU & unique audio $\rightarrow$ unique wrong text & one-off mismatches \\
RR & repeated wrong audio--text pairs & duplicated bad examples \\
RU & repeated audio $\rightarrow$ unique wrong text & inconsistent labels \\
UR & unique audio $\rightarrow$ repeated wrong text & template/label collapse \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

All conditions are generated from the same 120k Common Voice English subset.
Corrupted examples \emph{substitute} clean examples, so every training set keeps
the same total size. At 64\% corruption, 76.8k examples are replaced by
mismatched pairs. RR, RU, and UR use 10 repeated pairs, sources, or targets,
respectively; UU uses unique mismatches. Dataset generation is deterministic
from a fixed seed.

\subsection{Whisper adaptation}
We adapt \texttt{openai/whisper-large-v3} with LoRA using the same 64\%
training recipe for all four structures: five epochs, learning rate
$5\times10^{-5}$, LoRA rank 32, scaling 64, and identical target modules. The
analysis uses the latest complete near-final adapters available for each run
(9,375--10,000 steps, with the UU final adapter). This small checkpoint-step
difference is disclosed as a limitation; the LoRA architecture and data size
are matched across the primary comparison.

The main evaluation uses the same 300 held-out Common Voice utterances for all
models. Each utterance is decoded once in its original form and once after a
fixed full-utterance Gaussian-noise perturbation with absolute waveform
amplitude 0.5, clipped to $[-1,1]$. The perturbation is a diagnostic stress test,
not a simulation of a particular deployment SNR. Its purpose is to reduce
acoustic constraint and expose how the adapted model behaves when the signal is
uncertain.

\subsection{Failure signatures}
We measure four complementary properties.

\textbf{Recognition error.} WER is computed with one frozen normalization
pipeline for all conditions.

\textbf{LM-relative plausibility.} Qwen3-0.6B scores each hypothesis and its
reference. With $s(z)=\exp(-\overline{\mathrm{NLL}}(z))$, we report
$P_Q=\min(1,s(\hat y)/(s(y)+10^{-8}))$. This is an evaluation-only,
reference-normalized plausibility measure, not a reference-free detector.

\textbf{Hallucination-like diagnostic.} We use the previously frozen
high-WER/high-$P_Q$ criterion $H_Q$ to identify severe but linguistically
plausible failures. Thresholds are fixed before the present structure
comparison. Because repetition can itself produce high plausibility, we also
report NonRep-$H_Q$, which excludes any output containing a repeated trigram or
four-gram. Neither quantity is interpreted as human hallucination prevalence.

\textbf{Repetition and concentration.} Rep34 indicates at least one repeated
trigram or four-gram. Top-1 mass is the fraction of the evaluation set mapped to
the single most frequent normalized hypothesis.

\textbf{Corrupted-target provenance.} To test whether a failure is directly
traceable to bad supervision, each generated output is compared only with the
\emph{deliberately corrupted targets} from the corresponding training set.
Using word 1--3-gram TF--IDF cosine similarity, we report exact reuse, near reuse
(similarity $\geq0.85$), and mean nearest-target similarity. Provenance is
reported both over all examples and over high-error examples (WER above the
frozen high-error threshold).

\section{Results}
\subsection{Matched error prevalence yields different failure phenotypes}

more destructive regime, with WER 0.529, substantial repetition, and direct
target reuse. Under matched acoustic stress, however, all four models diverge
sharply.

\subsection{RU: plausible non-repetitive failure without target copying}
RU produces the clearest non-attractor regime. Under stress, its mean
LM-relative plausibility remains 0.857 and 78.0\% of outputs satisfy $H_Q$.
Crucially, 76.0\% still satisfy the criterion after repetition is excluded. Yet
only 0.7\% of high-error outputs are near matches to deliberately corrupted
training targets. The behavior therefore cannot be explained by simple
regurgitation of a small set of erroneous labels.

This is the practical risk of inconsistent supervision: the model can acquire a
strong tendency to produce fluent high-error text when acoustics weaken even
though those failures are not directly copied from the corrupted targets.

\subsection{UR: a training-derived target-attractor regime}
UR exhibits the opposite phenotype. Under stress, Rep34 reaches 61.3\%, and
95.3\% of high-error outputs are near matches to deliberately corrupted training
targets. Their mean nearest-target similarity is 0.921. The clean condition
already shows the same direction---50.0\% near reuse among high-error examples---
indicating that repeated erroneous targets are sufficiently strong to alter
ordinary recognition before the stress test is applied.

This provides direct provenance evidence for a target-attractor failure regime:
when many unrelated audio examples repeatedly reinforce a small set of wrong
transcripts, those transcripts become recurrent outputs when the model is
uncertain. The result is stronger than an association between repetition and
noise because the generated strings can be traced back to the intentionally
corrupted training targets.

\subsection{RR: concentrated collapse without corrupted-target reuse}
RR provides an important counterexample to the idea that output concentration
necessarily indicates memorization of bad labels. Under stress, the Qwen ratio
falls to 0.335 and $H_Q$ remains only 26.0\%, but Top-1 output mass reaches
52.0\%. Despite this collapse, exact and near corrupted-target reuse are zero.
Thus RR creates a restricted output distribution dominated by a generic
attractor, but not by direct reproduction of the deliberately corrupted labels.

\subsection{UU: an intermediate phenotype}
UU reaches $H_Q=67.0\%$ under stress, but only 43.7\% remains after repetitive
outputs are removed; Rep34 is 23.7\%. Like RU, it exhibits essentially no direct
target reuse (0.3\% among high-error examples), but its stressed failures are
less purely non-repetitive than RU. The four conditions therefore do not form a
single severity ordering. Instead, they occupy different regions of a failure
space defined by plausibility, repetition, concentration, and training-target
provenance.

\subsection{WER alone does not reveal the risk}
The clean comparison illustrates why an aggregate recognition metric is
insufficient. RR, RU, and UU have nearly identical WER around 0.15, yet under the
same stress they diverge into low-plausibility concentration (RR), highly
plausible non-repetitive failure (RU), and a mixed hallucination/repetition
regime (UU). Conversely, UR is visibly harmful even in clean WER, but WER alone
does not reveal that its distinctive mechanism is repeated-target reuse.

The point is therefore not that WER is uninformative. It is that WER measures
\emph{how wrong} a transcription is, whereas the structure audit identifies
\emph{how the model is wrong} and whether the failure is traceable to the
adaptation data.

\section{External Human Grounding with HALAS}
The controlled stress experiment intentionally amplifies failure tendencies and
its automatic diagnostic should not be confused with human hallucination
ground truth. We therefore use the official HALAS test split
\cite{baranski2026halas} as an external grounding set. HALAS contains human
annotations of hallucination and looping on naturally occurring Earnings22
speech. We analyze 4,990 released predictions from seven ASR systems without
rerunning inference.

Human hallucination is present in 22.9\% of the analyzed predictions. WER alone
discriminates human hallucination with ROC-AUC 0.761 and average precision
0.496: large error is informative, but not sufficient to identify whether an
output is hallucinatory. Repetition transfers more specifically. Rep34 occurs in
69.0\% of human-labeled looping outputs but only 1.76\% of non-looping outputs
(Fisher odds ratio 123.8, $p=6.56\times10^{-53}$). This supports treating
repetition as a separate human-recognizable failure component rather than
folding it into a generic hallucination rate.

HALAS does not human-label our stressed Common Voice outputs, so it cannot
validate the absolute $H_Q$ rates in Table~\ref{tab:riskmap64}. Its role is
narrower: it confirms that lexical error, hallucination, and looping are
empirically distinct on natural speech, motivating the multi-phenotype audit
used in the controlled experiment.

The controlled conditions are useful only if they lead to actionable checks. We
therefore map each observed structure to inexpensive data- and model-level
audits in Table~\ref{tab:audit}.

The central recommendation is simple: reporting that an adaptation set contains
``$x$\% noisy labels'' is not enough. At minimum, practitioners should also
inspect source duplication, target concentration, duplicated pairs, and
source--target consistency. After adaptation, WER should be complemented by a
small fixed acoustic-stress audit and, when recurrent failures appear, by
comparison against adaptation targets.

These checks target different problems. Deduplication can address repeated
pairs but not unique mismatches. Target-frequency filtering can detect template
collapse but not inconsistent labels for repeated audio. Conversely,
audio--text consistency filtering is necessary for one-off mismatches that do
not leave an obvious repeated-text signature.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Error prevalence and error structure are separate risk factors}
The primary experiment holds corruption prevalence fixed and changes only the
source/target repetition pattern. The resulting failure regimes are sufficiently
different that a single scalar label-noise rate cannot describe them. This is
particularly clear in the RU--UR contrast: RU produces high-plausibility,
non-repetitive failures with negligible label reuse, while UR produces a severe
repetition regime directly traceable to corrupted targets.

\subsection{Memorization is one route to failure, not the only one}
UR provides a transparent memorization-like pathway: repeated wrong targets
become decoding attractors. RU shows that this explanation is incomplete.
Despite a 78.0\% hallucination-like diagnostic rate under stress, the generated
errors almost never closely match corrupted labels. Erroneous supervision can
therefore alter failure behavior without producing obvious verbatim or
near-verbatim target reuse.

We deliberately describe RU as a non-attractor failure regime rather than
claiming a complete internal mechanism. Establishing whether it reflects a
change in acoustic--linguistic weighting, decoder prior strength, or another
representation-level effect requires mechanistic analysis beyond the present
paper.

\subsection{Why acoustic stress is useful}
The stress test is not intended to estimate deployment hallucination prevalence.
Its value is diagnostic: RR, RU, and UU look similar on clean WER but separate
strongly once acoustic evidence is weakened. A small matched stress battery can
therefore expose adaptation risks that remain latent during standard clean-speech
evaluation.

\section{Limitations}
The 64\% corruption rate is deliberately severe. It isolates structural effects
and produces sufficient failures for phenotype analysis, but it is not intended
as an estimate of realistic pseudo-label error prevalence. A dose-response study
at lower matched corruption levels is important future work.

The four corruption conditions are simplified interventions. Real adaptation
data can contain several structures simultaneously, and our practical analogues
should therefore be interpreted as auditing hypotheses rather than exact models
of production datasets.

The analysis focuses on Whisper-large-v3 with LoRA adaptation and English Common
Voice. The four primary adapters share the same LoRA configuration and training
recipe, but the surviving near-final checkpoints differ slightly in training
step (9,375--10,000 steps, with one final adapter). Replication with exactly
matched checkpoints and another model family would strengthen causal claims.

Finally, $H_Q$ is an automatic diagnostic, not human hallucination ground truth.
HALAS externally supports the need to separate lexical error, hallucination, and
looping, but does not contain judgments for our exact stressed outputs. We
therefore base our strongest mechanistic claim on direct corrupted-target
provenance rather than on the absolute $H_Q$ rate.

\section{Conclusion}
Not all training errors are equally dangerous for ASR. At the same 64\%
corruption prevalence, changing only the source/target repetition structure
produces qualitatively different Whisper failure phenotypes. RU yields fluent,
mostly non-repetitive high-error output without direct copying of corrupted
labels; UR creates a severe target-attractor regime in which 95.3\% of high-error
stressed outputs closely match bad training targets; RR produces concentrated
generic collapse without such reuse; and UU occupies an intermediate regime.

These results change the practical question from ``How noisy is the training
set?'' to ``What structure do its errors have, and which failure mode should we
audit for?'' ASR adaptation pipelines should therefore characterize source
repetition, target concentration, pair duplication, and alignment consistency,
and should evaluate adapted checkpoints for failure type in addition to WER.
